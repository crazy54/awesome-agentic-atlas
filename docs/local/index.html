      The three ideas that explain everything

      What’s a quantisation (Q4, Q8)?

      A model is billions of numbers. Quantising stores each one with fewer digits, the way a JPEG shrinks a photo. Q8 keeps 8 bits a number, about half the original size, and most people can’t tell it from the original. Q4 is under a third of the size and slightly worse. Below about 3 bits, answers get noticeably worse. We pick the best version that runs well on your machine, and we say when we had to squeeze one.

      Why does context length cost memory?

      Context is how much the model can keep in mind at once: your conversation so far, or a document you paste in. It sets aside memory for that up front, a little for every word it might hold, and how much varies a lot between models: Llama 3.1 8B needs about 130 MB per 1,000 tokens, while Qwen3.5 9B needs about 33 MB, because most of its layers keep no such memory. We assume about 8,000 tokens (around 12 pages), which is plenty for a chat. Pasting in a whole book needs much more, and on a small machine that can be the difference between fitting and not. “Show the numbers” gives each model’s own figure.

      What does “offload” mean?

      A graphics card has its own fast memory. If a model doesn’t fit in it, part can live in your ordinary RAM instead and run on the main processor. For most models that is much slower, because every word waits for the slow part: a big 70B model split this way writes about 2 words a second. Models called “mixture of experts” (names with A3B or A4B in them) are the exception. They only use a small part of themselves for each word, so the part kept in RAM is touched lightly, and they often still run well.

      Why does my Mac or handheld say less than its memory?

      Macs, Steam Decks and similar machines share one pool of memory between the processor and the graphics. That’s great for AI, because the graphics can use most of it, but the system keeps some for itself. On a Mac, macOS lets the graphics use two-thirds of the memory by default on Macs up to 32 GB, and three-quarters on bigger ones.

      What’s a token?

      The piece of text a model reads and writes at a time: usually part of a word. One English word is about 1.3 tokens, so 10 tokens a second is about 7 or 8 words a second, faster than most people read.

      How we worked these numbers out

      A model needs its download size, plus memory for its context, plus a little working space. Your machine offers a share of its memory, by one rule for each kind of machine: what a graphics card keeps for its driver and the screen, what macOS lets the graphics use, what Windows keeps for itself. Speed comes from how fast that memory can be read, because writing each word means reading the active part of the model once. The method is fitted to 144 published speed measurements across Macs, NVIDIA, AMD and Intel graphics, handhelds and processors, and lands within about 5% of them typically and 17% at worst for nine in ten. Real speed also depends on the app, drivers and settings, so every speed is shown as a range. The method is written up with its sources.
